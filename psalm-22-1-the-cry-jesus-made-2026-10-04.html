Psalm 22:1 — The Cry Jesus Made on Purpose

When Jesus cried these words from the cross, he wasn’t expressing defeat — he was pointing us to the whole story.

"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, so far from my cries of anguish?" — Psalm 22:1

▶ Listen in English

Of all the things Jesus could have said in his final hours, he chose a question. “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” It sounds like the end of faith — a man broken on a cross, crying into silence. And for centuries, many have read it that way: proof that even Jesus felt completely abandoned by God. But if we stop there, we’ve only heard half the story, and we’ve missed the extraordinary thing Jesus was actually doing.

Psalm 22 is a song David wrote in the middle of his own agony — surrounded by enemies, body wasting away, mocked by those who watched him suffer. It opens in raw, unfiltered despair. But then something shifts. By verse 24, David writes: “For he has not despised or scorned the suffering of the afflicted one; he has not hidden his face from him but has listened to his cry for help.” The psalm that begins in abandonment ends in trust, then in triumph — with future generations being told what God has done. The whole arc of the psalm moves from “why have you forsaken me” to “he has done it.”

When Jesus quoted the first verse from the cross, he was doing what every Jewish rabbi did with scripture — he was citing the opening line to invoke the entire passage in the minds of his listeners. He wasn’t just expressing anguish. He was saying: I am living this psalm, all the way through. And if you know how it ends, you know I am not finished. The cross was not the silencing of God’s voice. It was the fulfillment of an ancient song that always promised a morning after the darkest night.

So when you find yourself at the beginning of Psalm 22 — genuinely asking why God feels so far away — know this: you are not out of the psalm yet. The question is honest. The silence is real. And the God who heard David’s cry, and who raised Jesus from the pit of death, has not stopped listening. The lament is not the last word. It never was.

What People Think It Means

Most people assume that when Jesus cried “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” from the cross, he was expressing total spiritual abandonment — that in that moment, God the Father turned away from his Son and left him utterly alone. This reading frames the cry as a moment of divine separation, an expression of defeat or despair in the face of bearing humanity’s sin.

What It Actually Means

Jesus was quoting the opening line of Psalm 22, a practice that in Jewish tradition called the entire text to mind. This was intentional and deeply meaningful — Psalm 22 begins in desperate lament but ends in confident praise and ultimate vindication. By citing the first verse, Jesus wasn’t editing the psalm down to its darkest line; he was claiming the whole arc of the song. The psalm predicts details of his crucifixion with startling accuracy and closes with the declaration that God “has done it” — a phrase echoed in Jesus’s final breath when he said “It is finished.” The cry was not defeat. It was proclamation.

How to Apply It

When life hands you a Psalm 22 kind of moment — when God feels distant, silent, or indifferent — you don’t have to pretend otherwise. The psalm gives you permission to say exactly that out loud, honestly and even loudly. But it also invites you to hold on: the silence is not the final verse. Lament and faith are not opposites; in Scripture, they often live in the same breath.

En Español

Psalm 22:1 — The Cry Jesus Made on Purpose

▶ Escuchar en Español

De todas las cosas que Jesús pudo haber dicho en sus últimas horas, eligió una pregunta. “Dios mío, Dios mío, ¿por qué me has abandonado?” Suena como el fin de la fe: un hombre destrozado en una cruz, clamando al silencio. Y durante siglos, muchos lo han leído así: prueba de que incluso Jesús se sintió completamente abandonado por Dios. Pero si nos detenemos ahí, solo hemos escuchado la mitad de la historia, y nos hemos perdido lo extraordinario que Jesús estaba haciendo realmente.

El Salmo 22 es una canción que David escribió en medio de su propia agonía: rodeado de enemigos, con el cuerpo desfalleciente, burlado por quienes lo observaban sufrir. Comienza en desesperación cruda y sin filtros. Pero luego algo cambia. En el versículo 24, David escribe: “Porque no menospreció ni abomino la aflicción del afligido, ni le ocultó su rostro; sino que cuando clamó a él, le oyó.” El salmo que comienza en el abandono termina en confianza y luego en triunfo — con las generaciones futuras siendo informadas de lo que Dios ha hecho. Todo el arco del salmo avanza desde “por qué me has abandonado” hasta “lo ha consumado”.

Cuando Jesús citó el primer versículo desde la cruz, estaba haciendo lo que todo rabino judío hacía con las Escrituras: citaba la línea inicial para invocar el pasaje completo en la mente de sus oyentes. No solo estaba expresando angustia. Estaba diciendo: Estoy viviendo este salmo, de principio a fin. Y si sabes cómo termina, sabes que yo no he terminado. La cruz no fue el silenciamiento de la voz de Dios. Fue el cumplimiento de una canción antigua que siempre prometió una mañana después de la noche más oscura.

Entonces, cuando te encuentres al principio del Salmo 22, preguntando honestamente por qué Dios parece tan lejano, sabe esto: aún no has salido del salmo. La pregunta es honesta. El silencio es real. Y el Dios que escuchó el clamor de David, y que resucitó a Jesús del abismo de la muerte, no ha dejado de escuchar. El lamento no es la última palabra. Nunca lo fue.
