Isaiah 40:31 — The Wait That Isn't Waiting

The Hebrew word for 'wait' means to entwine — not passive sitting, but active, braided dependence on God.

"But those who wait on the Lord shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles, they shall run and not be weary, they shall walk and not faint." — Isaiah 40:31

▶ Listen in English

When life stalls — when the diagnosis lingers, the door stays closed, the relationship doesn't heal — we reach for this verse like a life raft. 'Those who wait on the Lord shall renew their strength.' We paste it on cards, we whisper it in waiting rooms. But if we're honest, sometimes it feels like a polite way of saying: hang on and do nothing.

There's a word beneath the English that changes everything. The Hebrew word translated 'wait' here is qavah — and it doesn't picture someone sitting still. It pictures cords being twisted together, braided, wound tight. Sailors used it for rope-making. To 'wait on the Lord' in the biblical sense is to entwine yourself with God — to wrap your life around His, to lean into Him with your full weight. It's not passive. It's one of the most active things a person can do.

Isaiah 40 was written for a broken people. Israel was in exile — displaced, exhausted, wondering if God had forgotten them. The prophet doesn't offer them an escape plan. He offers them God Himself: the One who 'does not faint or grow weary' (v. 28), who gives power to the weak and strength to the one who has none. The eagles' wings, the running without weariness — these aren't promises for the spiritually strong. They're for people who have nothing left, who stop trying to generate their own momentum and let themselves be held.

This is what it looks like to wait on God: returning to prayer when you don't want to, choosing a small act of trust when the big ones feel impossible, letting yourself be carried when you cannot walk. Waiting on the Lord is not inactivity. It is the deepest kind of hope — the kind that wraps itself around a God who will not let you go, even in the dark.

What People Think It Means

Most people hear 'wait on the Lord' as spiritual advice to sit still and do nothing — to be patient in a holding pattern until circumstances change. We use it to justify inaction, or offer it to others as comfort when we have no better answers. At its worst, it becomes a pious-sounding way of saying: give up trying.

What It Actually Means

The Hebrew word is qavah (קָוָה), rooted in the image of cords being twisted and braided together into rope. To wait on the Lord is to entwine yourself with Him — actively orienting your whole being toward God in hopeful dependence. Isaiah 40 was addressed to Israelites in Babylonian exile, bone-weary and demoralized. God's answer to their exhaustion is not a command to be passive but an invitation to draw strength from His inexhaustible nature (v. 28). The imagery of eagles, runners, and walkers describes three levels of endurance — extraordinary, sustained, and everyday — all sourced in this active, braided trust.

How to Apply It

Waiting on God looks like returning to prayer when you have no words, choosing one small act of trust in a dark season, allowing yourself to be held when you cannot move forward under your own power. The strength Isaiah promises is not a feeling of renewal — it's the quiet, stubborn reality of being held by One who never grows weary.

En Español

Isaiah 40:31 — The Wait That Isn't Waiting

▶ Escuchar en Español

Cuando la vida se detiene — cuando el diagnóstico persiste, la puerta sigue cerrada, la relación no sana — nos aferramos a este versículo como a un salvavidas. 'Los que esperan en el Señor renovarán sus fuerzas.' Lo ponemos en tarjetas, lo susurramos en salas de espera. Pero si somos honestos, a veces parece una manera educada de decir: aguanta y no hagas nada.

Hay una palabra debajo del español que lo cambia todo. La palabra hebrea traducida como 'esperar' es qavah — y no describe a alguien sentado quieto. Describe cuerdas que se entrelazan, se trenzan, se enrollan con fuerza. Los marineros la usaban para hacer sogas. 'Esperar en el Señor' en el sentido bíblico significa entrelazarse con Dios — envolver tu vida alrededor de la Suya, apoyarte en Él con todo tu peso. No es pasivo. Es una de las cosas más activas que una persona puede hacer.

Isaías 40 fue escrito para un pueblo quebrantado. Israel estaba en el exilio — desplazado, agotado, preguntándose si Dios los había olvidado. El profeta no les ofrece un plan de escape. Les ofrece a Dios mismo: Aquel que 'no se cansa ni se fatiga' (v. 28), que da fuerza al débil y fortalece al que no tiene fuerzas. Las alas de águila, el correr sin cansancio — estas no son promesas para los espiritualmente fuertes. Son para personas que ya no tienen nada, que dejan de intentar generar su propio impulso y se permiten ser sostenidas.

Así es como se ve esperar en Dios: volver a la oración cuando no tienes ganas, elegir un pequeño acto de confianza cuando los grandes se sienten imposibles, dejarte llevar cuando no puedes caminar. Esperar en el Señor no es inactividad. Es la forma más profunda de esperanza — la que se aferra a un Dios que no te soltará, ni siquiera en la oscuridad.
