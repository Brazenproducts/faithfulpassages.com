2 Chronicles 20:12 — No Idea What to Do

Jehoshaphat faced three armies with no plan and no time — and offered his helplessness to God as a prayer. Here's what that gives us.

"We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you."

— 2 Chronicles 20:12

▶ Listen in English

There's a moment in 2 Chronicles 20 that doesn't get nearly enough attention. Jehoshaphat, king of Judah, has just received word that three armies are marching toward Jerusalem. Not one rival city. Three armies. And there's no military alliance on speed dial, no wall high enough, no strategy that makes the math work out in his favor.

What he does next is remarkable — not because he rallies his troops or calls for the swords, but because he calls the entire nation together to fast and pray. He stands in the temple in front of everyone, and he does not pretend to be brave. He does not claim a victory he doesn't feel. He prays one of the most disarmingly honest prayers in all of scripture: "We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you."

That's it. No confident declaration. No five-step plan handed to God for his approval. Just the raw admission of bewilderment — and a single thread of trust: we are looking at you, even if we can't see what you're doing. In the original Hebrew, the phrase "our eyes are on you" carries the weight of desperate attention — the way you watch someone's face when you're hoping they'll tell you it's going to be okay.

Most of us have been trained to pray with certainty. To bring our requests with faith, to expect, to declare. And there is real beauty in bold prayer. But there are also seasons — and most of us know them — when confidence feels like performance. When the honest answer to "what are you trusting God for?" is: I don't know. I genuinely don't know what to ask for, because I don't understand what's happening. Jehoshaphat's prayer is for exactly those moments. It doesn't require you to have figured anything out. It only asks that you keep your eyes somewhere. And if you're going to look anywhere when everything is closing in, looking there might be the only prayer your season will allow.

What People Often Think

Most people assume that strong faith means confident prayer — that you're supposed to come to God already knowing what you want and believing you'll get it. The phrase 'I don't know what to do' sounds like the opposite of faith. It sounds like giving up. So this verse gets almost no airtime in churches that emphasize victorious, declarative Christianity.

What It Actually Means

Jehoshaphat's prayer comes in the context of a genuine military crisis — three enemy armies converging on Jerusalem with no human solution in sight. Rather than performing confidence he didn't have, he gathered the nation, fasted, and offered his helplessness as the prayer itself. In the Hebrew text, the posture of turning one's eyes to God while admitting complete blindness is not weakness — it's the deepest expression of trust available. The miracle that follows in the chapter is secondary; the turning point is the confession that comes before it.

How to Apply It

If you're in a season where you genuinely don't know what to do — in your relationship, your health, your work, your grief — this verse gives you permission to stop performing certainty. Bring the confusion itself. 'I don't know what to do, but my eyes are on you' may be the most faithful prayer your season will allow. You don't have to figure it out first.
